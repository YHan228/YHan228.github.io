"What is God?" is a question that recurs throughout the history of philosophy. Some of its answers are not descriptions of a transcendent being but assertions of *identity* about the whole of existence — that all of existence simply *is* itself, with no higher substance looking down on it. This essay compares how two historically independent and formally almost opposite traditions handle one such assertion: Spinoza's seventeenth-century derivation of "God, or Nature" (*Deus sive Natura*) in the *Ethics*, set out in the manner of Euclidean geometry; and the medieval Japanese doctrine of original enlightenment (本覚, *hongaku*), formed through centuries of hermeneutic accumulation, with its claim that "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" (凡夫即仏). The two set out from entirely different starting points and arrive at structurally similar conclusions. But what deserves more attention, perhaps, is the *manner* in which each arrives. This essay tries to locate where this type of identity-assertion stands once it is put under strict scrutiny.

## 1. God, or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*)

The *Ethics* (*Ethica Ordine Geometrico Demonstrata*), published after Spinoza's death in 1677, organizes its threads — definitions, axioms, corollaries — in the manner of Euclid's *Elements*, and from this rigorous form derives a view that was strikingly heterodox in its day: that God is the sole substance; that God is identical with Nature; and that all individuals are modes of that one substance. It answered Descartes's dualism of thought and extension, and offered seventeenth-century Europe a third path out of the dilemma of having to choose between a personal God and atheism — namely an impersonal, all-encompassing God-Nature. Setting aside the parts not directly relevant to the core, the line of argument runs roughly as follows.

**The implicit constitutive principle:** the Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR). Every distinction must have a sufficient ground; there are no unexplained "brute facts".

**Definitions:**

- D3 — Substance (*substantia*): that which is in itself and is conceived through itself; whose concept does not depend on the concept of any other thing.
- D4 — Attribute (*attributum*): that which the intellect perceives of substance as constituting its essence (*id quod intellectus de substantia percipit, tanquam ejusdem essentiam constituens*).
- D5 — Mode (*modus*): an affection of substance; or that which is in another and is conceived through another.
- D6 — God (*Deus*): an absolutely infinite being, a substance consisting of infinitely many attributes.

**Axioms:**

- A1: Everything that is, is either in itself or in another.
- A4: The cognition of an effect depends on, and involves, the cognition of its cause.

**The core chain of propositions:**

- **P5**: There cannot be two substances sharing the same attribute.

> Proof: Suppose there are two substances A and B; there must be some ground on which to distinguish them. The ground of distinction is either a difference in attribute or a difference in mode. If it is a difference in attribute, then A and B already differ at the level of attribute — meaning they do not "share the same attribute", and P5 holds. If it is a difference in mode — but a mode is an affection of substance (D5), determined by the substance rather than constituting its essence, and so cannot serve as a ground of distinction *between substances*. Hence the only viable distinction is by attribute, which rules out "sharing the same attribute".

- **P14**: No substance can exist other than God.

> By D6, God is absolutely infinite and entails all possible attributes. If there were another substance, it would have to have some attribute. But every attribute is already among God's infinite attributes. So the other substance would have to share some attribute with God, violating P5. Hence there is no substance other than God.

- **P15**: Whatever is, is in God.

> From P14 and A1.

- **P29, Scholium**: *Natura naturans* (Nature naturing) and *Natura naturata* (Nature natured) are two aspects of one and the same substance. The former is God as active producer; the latter is God as the totality of the modes produced.

Conclusion: the one substance is God; God is Nature (*Deus sive Natura*); and all individuals are modes of that substance.

Looking back at the definitions of this system, however, one notices something: "substance" — a word ordinarily used to refer to any of the individual things existing in reality — under Spinoza's definition cannot refer to any concrete thing at all. Any object of ordinary human cognition, even a stone or a pool of water, requires further descriptive concepts in order to be defined. A Spinozan substance possesses **self-completeness**, and that property can only belong to something all-encompassing. Combined with the definition of God as an absolutely infinite being containing all attributes, the identification of God with this all-encompassing absolute substance becomes all but inevitable.

What this reveals is that Spinoza's identity-conclusion is drawn out, with surprisingly little effort, by his idiosyncratic definition of substance. Completing that definition is the most important insight in his case for "God, or Nature" — and completing it is a problem that mere logical inference cannot solve; the introduction of a metaphysical commitment is unavoidable. Once that constructed set of definitions is accepted, the conclusion is formally complete.

What is striking is that nearly a thousand years before Spinoza, a current within Buddhism had already advanced, in China, a similar insight-based view — one that would gradually develop, in Japan, into what is called *hongaku* thought.

## 2. The Ordinary Person Is, As Such, the Buddha

*Hongaku* thought is a hermeneutic accumulation that ran for centuries and spanned two civilizations. It was transmitted mainly within the Tendai school through secret **oral transmission** (口伝, *kuden*) and **paper slips** (切紙, *kirikami*), and only gradually compiled into written texts retrospectively attributed to ancient authorities.

The term "*hongaku*" (original enlightenment) first appears in the *Awakening of Faith in the Mahāyāna* (大乗起信論), a sixth-century text composed in China but attributed to India. It says:

> 所言覺義者，謂心體離念。離念相者，等虛空界，無所不遍，法界一相，即是如來平等法身。依此法身，說名本覺。何以故？本覺義者，對始覺義說，以始覺者，即同本覺。

That is: "enlightenment" is the recognition that the essence of mind is from the outset free of discursive thought; this essence is in fact omnipresent, like empty space; the entire dharma-realm reduces to a single mark, and that mark is the Tathāgata's undifferentiated *dharmakāya*, i.e. "original enlightenment". The "initial enlightenment" attained through practice is identical to the original enlightenment one had all along.

This opening insight already contains most of what *hongaku* thought would become, though it still rests on a distinction between mind-essence and discursive thought, and still presupposes practice.

Monk Zhiyi (538–597), in the *Mohe Zhiguan* (摩訶止観), established "the three thousand realms in a single thought-moment" (一念三千): a single moment of mind contains all three thousand worlds. From the seventh to the ninth centuries, the Tiantai and Huayan schools developed this ontological direction further, advancing "the unobstructed interpenetration of all phenomena" (事事無礙), "one is all, all is one" (一即一切、一切即一), and "even the insentient possess [Buddha-]nature" (無情有性). These form the core of *hongaku* thought; but at this stage they remain doctrine, while actual practice still proceeds gradually through precepts, meditation, and stages.

In China, amid the fierce competition of Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism, this ontological current saw little further development. Transmitted to Japan, however, it was interpreted further and pushed to its limit. In the ninth century, the early-Heian Tendai monk Annen (安然), in his *Shinjō sōmoku jōbutsu shiki* (斟定草木成仏私記), framed the signature line "grasses, trees, and the very land all attain Buddhahood" (草木国土悉皆成仏). Out of its interpretation came three important conclusions:

> **Non-duality of environment and subject** (依正不二): the "environment" (依) and the "subject" (正) were never two things to begin with.
>
> **The self-nature of the thing as such** (当体の自性): a thing's essence just *is* the way it is now manifesting, not some "true essence" hidden behind it.
>
> **Containing the Middle Way** (中道を具す): within "the three thousand realms in a single thought-moment", grasses too are not lacking; hence grasses also attain Buddhahood.

In Japan's medieval period (11th–14th centuries), facing the onset of the "Final Dharma age" (末法, *mappō*, reckoned from 1052), and coinciding with the Heian–Kamakura transition — the rise of the estate system and the warrior class, the decline of the old aristocracy and the waning of the court, compounded by natural disasters — Japan's despair at an impending "end of days" generated a fresh demand for "salvation". In this period, *hongaku* thought was finally consolidated into a few orally transmitted truths:

> **Birth-and-death is, as such, nirvāṇa; the afflictions are, as such, awakening; the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha; grasses and trees all attain Buddhahood.** (生死即涅槃、煩悩即菩提、凡夫即仏、草木悉皆成仏)

Note that "non-duality of environment and subject" is not "identity of environment and subject"; whereas the "即" (*soku*, "is, as such") in formulations like "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" can be read as strict identity, and it is that reading I adopt here.

To the Final Dharma age, *hongaku* thought's answer is this: salvation was never elsewhere; it is in the phenomenon of this very moment, here and now. There is no far shore to be reached. Many later schools of Japanese Buddhism were deeply marked by this idea, which in turn soaked into Zen, Noh, *waka*, linked verse, *haikai*, and the way of tea.

## 3. Reconstructing Original Enlightenment

Note that the original *hongaku* thought was built by **hermeneutic accumulation**, not deductive argument. Over several centuries, many thinker-monks, named and unnamed, contributed to its eventual form, continually interpreting and debating the lines left by their predecessors, ultimately attributing the result to ancient authorities, leaving behind a scattered literature whose details are hard to pin down.

Borrowing Spinoza's form, let us attempt a derivation of *hongaku*'s main propositions from definitions and axioms. At the same time, a derivation of this kind, grounded in a metaphysical commitment, is itself one expression of interpretation; from it something may emerge that diverges from the original meaning without straying too far, which is precisely what the scriptures' ambiguity permits.

### 3.1 Axioms and Definitions

We begin with three statements.

- A1 (Dependent origination, 緣起): the nature of any existent is wholly determined by its relations to other things; there is no non-dependently-originated existent.
- A2 (Emptiness of self-nature, 性空): from A1 — all things lack self-nature (*svabhāva*).
- A3 (Occurrent-ness): any existent is, at any moment, in some state.

Of these, A1 and A2 rest on the metaphysical commitments of the Buddhist worldview. A3 looks trivial; we accept it provisionally as a premise needing no argument, and return to it later.

### 3.2 Dissolving "the Buddha"

We need to rethink **"the Buddha".** Intuitively, any transcendent Buddha — of the kind that the historical development of Buddhism conferred upon them — seems to contradict the core claim "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha": if the Buddha has any ontological property the ordinary person lacks, then the ordinary person is, at least in that property, not the Buddha.

Let us try to derive this constraint from the axioms.

- **Theorem 1 (No ontological superiority):** For any dependently-originated existents X and Y, the substantive proposition "X is ontologically superior to Y" cannot be well-defined.

> Proof: Suppose "X is ontologically superior to Y" is a well-defined substantive proposition; then it states an ontological fact, and that fact requires some ground. By A1 + A2, X and Y lack the independent self-nature that could serve as the locus of a ground. So the ground cannot lie in X or Y themselves; it can lie only in one of three places: in the network of relations between X and Y, in some third thing common to both, or in no specific locus at all. If in the relational network, superiority degenerates into a conventional label, not an ontological fact. If in a third thing, that third thing is likewise dependently originated and, by A2, lacks self-nature, and so cannot serve as "the ontological ground". If in no locus at all, superiority has no substantive content. The three possibilities are exhaustive, and all are ruled out. Hence the proposition cannot be well-defined.

- **Theorem 2 (The dissolved Buddha):** Given A1 + A2, any definition of "the Buddha" consistent with A1 must be dissolutive — it cannot require any ontologically superior state.

> Proof: Suppose someone asserts a definition D\*: the Buddha = a being in some ontologically superior state X. Consider the ontological status of state X:
> If X is dependently originated, then by T1 the ontological-superiority relation between X and any other dependently-originated state Y cannot be well-defined; but D\* requires X to be ontologically superior to at least some other states (otherwise it is not "superior") — contradiction.
> If X is not dependently originated, it violates A1.
> By A1 these two cases are exhaustive and both ruled out, so D\* is inconsistent.

T2's output is negative — it tells us what "the Buddha" cannot be, not what it is. It even permits a more radical response: **having accepted T2, simply abandon the category "Buddha", since under our worldview it has no content.**

But let us first try to keep "the Buddha" as a meaningful category. To do so we must do more work on top of T2 — give "the Buddha" a positive content compatible with A1.

This requires two commitments, which we treat separately.

### 3.3 First Commitment: Ontological Ranking Is Inapplicable

T1 has already told us that ontological-ranking comparisons cannot be well-defined. Push this conclusion onto judgments about the ordinary person's state:

- **L1 (Non-penetration):** No existent's present state is penetrated by the judgment that "it ought to become some ontologically superior state".

> Proof: Suppose some existent X's present state were penetrated by the judgment "it ought to become Y". That judgment would require Y to be ontologically superior to X's present state. But by T1, such a superiority-judgment cannot be well-defined. So the judgment has no content. Hence X's present state is not penetrated by it.

But on closer inspection, L1's proof depends on T1, and T1's proof depends on an implicit meta-level rule. Its key step is: the proposition "X is ontologically superior to Y" *requires some ground*, and every possible ground is ruled out by A1 + A2. The requirement "requires some ground" was never stated; it is an implicit meta-rule:

- **A4:** Any substantive proposition about a thing's ontological status requires some definable ground in order to be well-defined.

This is a narrowly scoped, weakened PSR, restricted to attributions of ontological status.

Accept A4, and L1's proof is complete.

### 3.4 Second Commitment: The Identity of State and Reality

L1 tells us that no "ought-to-become" ontological norm penetrates the ordinary person's present state. But it does not tell us that the ordinary person's present state **is** their reality. A gap remains.

A thing could perfectly well satisfy L1 (no ontological-norm judgment penetrates it) and yet still have some unmanifested "true state" — its present manifestation being only part of its reality, the complete reality including unmanifested layers. This would let "the Buddha" possess unmanifested states that distinguish him from the ordinary person.

For *hongaku* thought to stand, we need an additional commitment:

- **Assertion C1 (Self-nature of the thing as such):** the state any existent presently manifests *is* its actual state.

C1 denies the phenomenon/thing-in-itself split, denies the possibility of any "unmanifested truth", and asserts that there is no gap between appearance and reality.

We try to derive C1 from the axioms.

Does it come from A3? A3 says "any existent is at any moment in some state"; C1 says "the presently manifested state is its actual state". The two are not equivalent. A3 asserts only the link between existent and state; it does not tell us whether that state is its "actual" state or merely its "manifested" one.

Can it be derived from A1 + A2? A2 says all phenomena lack self-nature, which means there is no reality "in the thing itself" independent of relations. But strictly, A2 rules out only *independent* self-nature; it does not directly rule out a *relational*, unmanifested state. C1 is stronger than A2.

C1 cannot be derived from the axioms in hand. It is an independent, strong commitment.

If we accept C1, we obtain the commitment that the ordinary person's present state *is* their actual state, with no hidden "true state".

### 3.5 Synthesis: Buddha-Nature and the Buddha

We now have two commitments from independent sources. L1 gives non-penetration — the ordinary person is not penetrated by ontological-norm judgments (resting on A1 + A2 + A4). C1 gives state–reality identity — the ordinary person's present state is their actual state (resting on an independent commitment). Together they let us define:

- D1 — Buddha-nature: **suchness, being-just-as-it-is** — the state a thing presently displays is its actual state (by C1), and is not penetrated by the second-order judgment "it ought to be Y" (by L1).

- D2 — Buddha: a being that fully realizes its own Buddha-nature.

From D1 + D2 + C1: the ordinary person's present state is their Buddha-nature; the ordinary person fully realizes their Buddha-nature; the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha.

### 3.6 The First "Stick-and-Shout"

Let us examine the meta-rule A4 more closely.

T1's proof lists three possible loci for a ground — the relational network, a common third thing, no locus at all — and rules each out via A1 + A2. But in substance, these three exclusions apply equally to **all** substantive propositions at the ontological level, not just to "X is ontologically superior to Y".

By exactly the same argument, what we have in fact proved is:

> **Theorem\*:** Given A1, A2, and A4, *all* substantive propositions about a thing's ontological status cannot be well-defined. This includes superiority relations, non-superiority relations, identity relations, difference relations, and any substantively meaningful ontological comparison or attribution.

Let us turn this finding back on the "ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" just obtained in §3.5.

"The ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" is a substantive proposition about ontological status: it asserts that the ordinary person and the Buddha are ontologically identical. By Theorem\*, this proposition cannot be well-defined.

L1 says not merely that "the ordinary person's present state is not penetrated by ontological norms"; what it actually says is that **"the present state is penetrated" and "is not penetrated", as ontological propositions, both have no content.**

And in D2, "Buddha = a being that *fully realizes* its own Buddha-nature", the phrase "fully realizes" is likewise an attribution of ontological sufficiency, and so, under the strict enforcement of A4, also cannot be well-defined.

> **Corollary:** Given A1, A2, and A4, the entire language of ontology, *as a tool for expressing substantive propositions*, is meaningless and yields no content.

It took us six sections to confirm: under ontological formal logic, a complete reconstruction of *hongaku* thought that does not violate its own worldview is impossible.

### 3.7 Retreating to Madhyamaka

Keeping §3.1, what remains available to us is precisely what the Madhyamaka school could do: by the tetralemma (四句, *catuṣkoṭi*) and *reductio*, lead the opponent of a given ontological proposition into the worldview of dependent-origination-and-emptiness, so that he sees, on his own, the negation of his own proposition.

Here, the "tetralemma" refers to:

> Things do not arise from themselves, nor from another, nor from both, nor without a cause; thus we know there is no [inherent] arising. (諸法不自生、亦不从他生、不共不无因、是故知无生)

This is a fourfold negation of the modes of *arising* (from self / from another / from both / without cause). In formal terms, it is isomorphic to the joint negation of the four possibilities $ X,\ \lnot X,\ X \land \lnot X,\ \lnot(X \lor \lnot X)$.

What matters is that Madhyamaka offers no positive conclusion on ontological-relation judgments, thereby avoiding the trap of falling into "everything is [merely] empty".

On this system, we must halt at "the ordinary person and the Buddha are incomparable", and can go no further in any sayable way.

*Hongaku* in part incorporates this negative conclusion of Madhyamaka, and in part inherits the *tathāgatagarbha* assertion that "sentient beings inherently possess Buddha-nature". But the simple sum of the two can still yield only the generic conclusions "incomparable" and "the ordinary person also has Buddha-nature".

### 3.8 The Second "Stick-and-Shout"

So then — historically, by whom, and in what manner, was the core articulation of *hongaku* thought completed?

It came to stand by several independent routes, a convergence of the threads in §2. But whether by way of Tendai or the *Awakening of Faith*, the *hongaku* conclusion was ultimately reached by a hermeneutic technique common to medieval Japan — the invention and rearrangement of texts, wordplay, numerical correspondence, reversal — rather than by logical argument.

Here I display one such hermeneutic performance: Dōgen's, in 1241. [^1]

[^1]: Though Dōgen himself held reservations about the strict *hongaku* conclusion. He continued to insist on the necessity of practice — person and Buddha are not strictly identical.

Writing the "Buddha-Nature" (佛性, *Busshō*) fascicle of the *Shōbōgenzō* at Kōshō-ji in Kyoto, Dōgen faced the famous line from the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*:

```
> 一切        衆生                  悉             有          佛性
> all  (sentient) beings   without exception   possess    Buddha-nature
```

i.e., all living beings possess Buddha-nature.

Relying on the ambiguity of Classical Chinese, which has no punctuation (phrasing is left to the interpreter) and lets a single character carry several grammatical roles at once, together with the elidability of the copula, he reread the line; precisely, he read "悉有" (*shitsuu*) — originally an adverb-plus-copula component — as a nominal phrase:

> 一切衆生、悉有佛性、如来常住、無有変易。\
> 悉有の言は衆生なり、群有なり。すなはち悉有は仏性なり。悉有の一悉を衆生といふ。 ("The word *shitsuu* — 'existence in its entirety' / 'whole-being' — is all beings; it is all-that-is. That is to say, existence in its entirety *is* Buddha-nature. One instantiation of whole-being is what we call all beings".)

The original line can then be parsed as:

```
> 一切        衆生                      悉有                      佛性
> all  (sentient) beings —— existence in its entirety —— Buddhic being-as-such
```

i.e.: all beings, [are] existence in its entirety, [(therefore) are] Buddha-nature. (A definition and an identity-judgment. The category traditionally restricted to sentient things — living beings — is by definition expanded to all of existence, and that whole of existence just *is* Buddha-nature.)

The key moves here are:

1. Expanding the category "sentient beings" to include the insentient, raising "even the insentient possess [Buddha-]nature" from an attribution to an identity.
2. Making "all beings" defined by and contained within "whole-being", with "whole-being" expressing itself through all beings.
3. Exploiting the ontological sense (*svabhāva*) that the character 性 had already taken on in the Chinese Buddhist translation tradition, to push "Buddha-nature" entirely out of the category of *attribute* and into the category of *mode of existence* (thus "Buddhic being-as-such").
4. Asserting that Buddha-nature just *is* the as-it-is mode of existence. Whole-being, as the totality of existence, at its mode of being, is Buddhic being-as-such; and all beings, as expressions of whole-being, likewise are Buddhic being-as-such.

The fourth move has already abolished the Buddha's transcendence, so that the next step — asserting "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" — is a maximal-limit corollary of this reading. Dōgen himself retained the reservation of practice-versus-non-practice; other *hongaku* currents went further and abolished that distinction.

The power of interpretation is now set loose. So let me, in turn, put forward a new punctuation — juxtaposing the concepts in silence, set side by side, unjudged — for interpretive contemplation:

```
> 一切。衆生。悉有。佛性。
> Everything. Every sentient being. Existence in its entirety. Buddhic being-as-such.
```

## 4. Same Within the Difference

This essay has introduced two views of the question "What is God?" — historically unrelated, formally opposed.

Spinoza argued by formal logic that God is the whole of Nature, not some personified being. Yet the rigorous form conceals a metaphysical commitment that does much of the work: in choosing that "substance" which cannot refer to any concrete thing in reality, the argument is already half won. And that substance has a deeper, un-arguable source: PSR as the constitutive principle. Any substance dependent on another would make some fact a brute fact; hence no substance can be dependent on another, and a self-existent substance must exist.

*Hongaku* thought, drawing on the Buddhist worldview and its scriptures, reached a similar insight through interpretive performance and assertion: the mode of being of all existence is possessed by the ordinary person, by grasses and trees, by earth and stone alike — and there is no transcendent ideal state to be reached; the ordinary person, the grasses and trees, the earth and stone, simply by existing, are already in that one true state. What our inquiry has found is that the dependence which lets this system's surface argument stand, namely A4, is itself PSR in its ontological guise; and this constitutive principle, being itself an ontological-status proposition, likewise cannot be well-defined, and so stands in contradiction with the very worldview it serves.

That *Hongaku* rests on un-argued assertion and interpretation is not, on this essay's reading, an essential defect, but a choice forced by the Buddhist worldview's fundamental refusal of ontological propositions. Either shout the insight aloud, or else stay with negation, or else fall silent. As for Spinoza, the argument it ran through is already contained in that concept of substance, itself reached without argument.

Any account, then, of the ontological identity of the human, of God, of the whole of existence, must introduce one or a set of un-argued commitments (here, PSR and the core cluster of definitions it generates), without which there is nothing to be said at all.

## 5. A Long and Distant Stream

By way of conclusion, this essay ventures the following: *hongaku* thought appears to have shaped almost the whole of Japanese cultural life from the medieval period onward, and to have molded Japan's distinctive aesthetic system — but its influence runs more at the level of meta-conceptual legitimacy, and the *way that aesthetic system was produced* is homologous with the way *hongaku* thought itself was produced.

From real landscape to dry landscape, from the exquisite to *wabi-sabi*, from the gilded-hall *tenmoku* bowl to the thatched-hut black *raku* bowl — many of the elements of Japanese aesthetics now famous the world over underwent, from the medieval period on, an upheaval that "turned black and white around". On the face of it, all this can be read as "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" extended onto objects. So: is *hongaku* thought the *positive* warrant for this reversal? I would argue that what it touches reaches, at most, the ontological status of the proposition: that fullness as a state is not ontologically superior to brokenness; and so too for the lush against the withered, the refined against the coarse, the Chinese import (唐物, *karamono*) against the native ware (和物, *wamono*). Each state is equally "just as it is"; no state ontologically dominates another. But "is fullness beautiful", "where is the beauty in native ware" — these are aesthetic questions, not ontological ones. So what *hongaku* actually does is *remove the ontological illegitimacy* that had been attached to brokenness. The Chinese import was prized and gazed upon because it "by nature" belonged to a superior culture; this order bound aesthetic preference and ontological fact together, so that the preference looked as if it rested on an unquestionable hierarchy. But demolition is the work of a single sentence; construction is not. Cancelling the hierarchy only clears a patch of ground — no one can say, out of nothing, *why* brokenness is beautiful.

What is the common thread? Several of the major transformations across the various domains of medieval-and-later Japan walked the same road: first sever the old order in one stroke, force the gaze onto the very thing that was deemed unworthy of being looked at — the plain, the silent, the broken — and then, slowly, grow new content in the cleared space. It is like Zen's "stick-and-shout" (棒喝): in an instant it severs the disciple's discursive mind, forcing out the "presence" (在場) of that moment. And the new beauty grows slowly out of the attention that this "presence" permits. It comes from beholding, contemplating, gazing, interpreting — and it requires the accumulated practice of several generations.

This is of a piece with what Sen no Rikyū did as the practitioner of this very method in the way of tea. Before him, the tea gathering was defined by lavish abundance and ostentatious display, by which the host paraded his wealth and rank. The two generations of Murata Jukō and Takeno Jōō had already put most of the spirit of *wabi*-tea into practice, yet were still negotiating within the old frame; the decisive reversal was the one Rikyū brought down. He reversed every step of the ritual: a small, dim tea room; a bamboo flower vase; a single flower; coming, finally, to rest on a purely black tea bowl that *must* be gazed at. Every aesthetic expectation of the guest was interrupted, high-handedly and without argument. These utterly unremarkable things were, for the first time, beheld in earnest.

That this method was invoked again and again in medieval Japan had its circumstances. An island country struck repeatedly by disaster; a medieval era of war and upheaval; a culture long overshadowed by an overwhelmingly vast mother-culture on the continent; and, theologically, the Final Dharma age entered in 1052. Japan needed a road out of the inherited order: one that could both settle that millennia-deep sense of "impermanence" (無常, *mujō*) and clear, outside continental culture, a place of its own to stand on. Reversal offered exactly that road: it need not first prove the new order superior — it need only first declare that the old order's hierarchy was empty all along. And, conveniently, the notions being reversed — abundance, refinement, the Chinese import — were almost all things at odds with "impermanence" and imported from the continent; so this method, distinctively, opened up the vast aesthetic space latent in the plain, the silent, the broken — a system still alive after more than five hundred years.

This un-arguable "stick-and-shout", then, can be seen as a structural method of Japanese cultural transformation from the medieval period onward. It comes down again and again, only wearing a different face each time: in the garden, sand and stone in place of real water; in the tea room, a black bowl in place of a golden one; in thought, the line "the ordinary person is, as such, the Buddha" — held up not by argument but by assertion and interpretation. The method is filed under the Zen tradition, but its origins must be quite tangled. The Buddha's "noble silence" before questions he would not answer (c. 6th century BCE), Zhuangzi's stance of drawing out doctrine through stories beyond ordinary sense (c. 3rd century BCE), the Japanese native experience of coping with "impermanence" across its history — each contains something answering to the "stick-and-shout".

The un-arguability of *hongaku* thought, which this essay has spent so many pages examining, is precisely one appearance of that method at the level of thought.
